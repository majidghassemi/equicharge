\begin{abstract}
Public electric vehicle charging is becoming everyday infrastructure at the same moment
it reaches lower-income drivers, and those drivers are the ones who most depend on it,
because they are least likely to have a private charger at home. A charging site draws
power through a fixed grid connection, so at busy hours it must ration, and operators
already price by tier through memberships, product tiers, and dynamic pricing that drivers
sort into by budget. We audit what a revenue-optimal allocation does under that scarcity
and find a distributional harm that is not obvious from the outside. Using an exact offline
solver as the audit instrument, on a real-data-calibrated simulator, we show that
revenue-optimal rationing systematically underserves the lowest-paying tier, and because
tier tracks income, housing tenure, and race, the effect is a disparate impact on
already-marginalized drivers rather than deliberate income discrimination. The central and
counterintuitive finding is about the fix. The intuitive equity remedy, a subsidy aimed at
the poorest drivers, does not remove the harm and can relocate it onto the middle tier. The
harm tracks the tiers' rank ordering by price and not how wide the spread is, so capping the top
tier barely helps and only removing the ordering, an income-neutral tariff, removes the harm, at
a cost that falls on the operator's revenue rather than on the total energy served. We show the harm is specific to power-scarce sites, which
lets a regulator target the remedy, and we show the harm is structural rather than a calibration
artifact, because it depends only on the rank ordering of the tiers and not on the magnitude of
the price gap between them, invariant to the willingness-to-pay elasticity we assume. We name the affected people,
the decision-makers who hold the levers, and the governance instruments those levers map to,
and we are explicit about whose perspectives our simulation cannot represent and how a model
of ability-to-pay could be misused. We release the audit instrument so operators, utilities,
and regulators can re-run it on their own charging data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Charging an electric vehicle away from home is not the same experience for everyone. The
drivers who most depend on public charging, renters and residents of multi-unit buildings
and lower-income households, are exactly the ones for whom a private home charger is out of
reach, and public charging availability and price already fall unevenly across income and
race \cite{hsu2021,khan2022,caulfield2022}. As electric vehicles move down the income
distribution, the question of who public charging serves well becomes a question of equity,
and it becomes one at the moment the infrastructure is being built out and priced, when the
rules are still soft.

This paper is a socio-technical audit of one such rule. A public charging site is a scarce
resource allocator. A single fast charger can request more than one hundred kilowatts, and a
site's grid connection is often rated well below the sum of what its chargers could draw at
once, so when several cars are present the operator must ration power among them. Most
controllers deployed in this setting optimize revenue, because revenue is easy to measure and
it pays for the site, and operators already differentiate price by tier through memberships,
subscription plans, product tiers such as fast versus level two, and dynamic and congestion
pricing. Drivers sort into these tiers by budget, and tier membership is documented to
correlate with income \cite{drehobl2020,hsu2021}. We ask what a revenue-optimal allocation
does to the people in the low-margin tiers when power binds, and we audit the answer with an
instrument that is exact rather than heuristic.

The finding is a distributional harm, and the reason it belongs at this venue is that the
harm is neither intended nor obvious, and the intuitive remedy for it is wrong. A
revenue-maximizing schedule under scarcity mechanically directs the limited power to the
higher-margin tiers and lets the lower-margin tiers leave with half-empty batteries. Because
the low-margin tiers are disproportionately lower-income, and because reliance on public
charging is itself concentrated among renters, multi-unit-dwelling residents, and drivers of
color, the effect is a disparate impact on marginalized drivers produced by a facially
neutral policy that never reads anyone's income. The operator does not discriminate. The
pricing structure does.

\paragraph{The counterintuitive finding, stated first.}
The equity remedy a policymaker reaches for first is a subsidy for the poorest drivers, and
it does not work. On our audit a subsidy targeted only at the lowest tier fails to close the
gap and, once it lifts the poorest tier off the bottom, relocates the shortfall onto the
middle tier rather than removing it. The reason is structural. What produces the harm is the
rank \emph{ordering} of the tiers by price, and the revenue optimum starves whichever tier is
strictly lowest, so capping the top tier barely helps and only removing the ordering, an
income-neutral tariff, removes the harm. The cost of doing so is paid in the operator's revenue
and not in the total energy delivered, because the grid limit fixes how much energy is served
and the choice of objective only decides who receives it. This is the shape of finding this venue exists to surface. An
audit that reveals an unintended distributional harm, shows that the obvious fix backfires,
and identifies the structural lever that actually works, together with who must pull it.

\paragraph{What we contribute.}
First, we ground the harmed group in observable, documented conditions rather than in a
modeling parameter, tying the tiers to home-charging access, housing tenure, and reliance on
public charging, and we test that the finding survives the uncertainty in that grounding.
Second, we characterize the harm and its structural cause precisely, the price of fairness is
a revenue cost, the harm tracks the tiers' price ordering rather than the spread magnitude, and
it is specific to power-scarce sites. Third, we situate the finding in the real decision-makers and governance
instruments that can act on it, rate regulation, low-income charging tariffs, and grid
connection sizing. Fourth, we release the audit instrument so any operator, utility, or
regulator can re-run the audit on their own arrival and pricing data. We treat an online
learned controller as a secondary question, whether an operator could realize the fairer
allocation online, and we report it briefly and honestly, because it is not the contribution.

\section{Background and normative commitments}

We situate the work in energy justice and distributive justice rather than in the machinery
of fair machine learning, because the object of study is a distribution of a basic service
across people and the question is which distribution is just, not which algorithm is fastest.

\paragraph{Energy justice.}
The energy justice literature organizes fairness in energy systems into distributional,
procedural, and recognition dimensions \cite{sovacool2014,jenkins2016}. Our audit is squarely
in the distributional dimension, it asks who gets the scarce good and who does not, and it
connects to the large body of work on energy burden and energy poverty, where low-income
households spend several times the income share on energy that higher-income households do
\cite{drehobl2020,bednar2020}. We are aware that a purely distributional audit leaves the
procedural and recognition dimensions unaddressed, that we have no participatory input from
affected drivers and no account of how they would frame the problem themselves, and we return
to this limit in Section~\ref{sec:reflexivity} rather than let the distributional frame stand
in for the whole of justice.

\paragraph{Why these fairness definitions, and what each commits to.}
Fairness here is contested, not a single number to optimize, and our audit instrument makes
the contest explicit by computing three different optimal allocations, each of which encodes
a different normative commitment. The revenue-optimal allocation is the status quo we audit,
it maximizes value-weighted delivered energy and encodes the commitment that willingness to
pay should govern access. The utilitarian allocation maximizes total satisfaction across
drivers and encodes the commitment that a unit of charging counts equally whoever receives
it. The maximin allocation maximizes the satisfaction of the worst-off tier and encodes the
Rawlsian difference principle, that an inequality is justified only if it improves the
position of the least advantaged \cite{rawls1971}. We report a strict-equality allocation as
well, which forces every tier to a common level, precisely to expose the difference between
equalizing and leveling down, a distinction the fairness literature insists on and a naive
maximin blurs. The choice among these is a value choice and we state it rather than smuggle it
in. When we say an intervention makes things fairer, we mean it under an explicitly named
objective, and we report what each objective costs the better-off tier so the reader can see
the value trade rather than take our word that it is free. We take the capability view, due to
Sen, that the right space in which to assess this is access to charging rather than money
paid, which is why our per-driver measure is the fraction of requested energy actually
delivered and not the revenue collected \cite{sen1999}. The inequality-aversion machinery we
use to interpolate between these positions, the Atkinson index and its equally distributed
equivalent and the one-parameter family of welfare functions, is standard and we use it as a
lens rather than as a novelty \cite{atkinson1970,mo2000,moulin2003}.

\paragraph{Disparate impact, used precisely.}
\label{sec:disparate}
We use the term disparate impact in its technical sense and we are careful about the
conditions it requires. Disparate impact refers to a facially neutral policy, one that does
not use a protected attribute, that nonetheless produces a disproportionate adverse effect on
a protected or marginalized group. Our revenue-optimal allocation is facially neutral in
exactly this sense, it optimizes margin-weighted energy and never observes any driver's
income, race, or housing status. The adverse effect on the low-margin tier is what the audit
measures. The claim that this is a disparate impact on a protected group, rather than only a
regressive outcome across price tiers, rests on the empirical chain we lay out and defend in
Section~\ref{sec:grounding}, tier tracks home-charging access, which tracks housing tenure and
multi-unit-dwelling residence, which track income and race. Where that chain is strong, the
legal and moral language of disparate impact applies. Where a reader finds the chain only
partially supported, the weaker and still-serious description is a regressive allocation or a
distributional inequity across ability to pay, and our structural findings hold under either
reading, because they concern the mechanism, the tiers' price ordering, which is the same
whichever label the effect carries.

\paragraph{Rate design.}
Because our sharpest lever is the tariff, we build on the applied economics of electricity
pricing, where the distributional incidence of nonlinear and time-varying pricing is studied
directly \cite{borenstein2012,burger2020}. That literature is what lets us say which realistic
tariff designs move the harm and which do not, and it is where our recommendation, an
income-neutral tariff that removes the price ordering across tiers, lives as an instrument a
utility regulator actually has.

\section{The setting and the harmed group}
\label{sec:grounding}

The credibility of a disparate-impact audit rests on whether the harmed group is real, so we
spend this section grounding it rather than assuming it.

\paragraph{A real-data-calibrated environment.}
We audit on Chargax, a simulator that models a station as a tree of power nodes under a hard
grid-connection limit \cite{ponse2025}. Time advances in five-minute steps and one episode is
a single day of 288 steps, and at each step a controller sets a discrete charging level for
every charger and for an on-site battery. The distributions the environment samples from are
empirical rather than synthetic, arrival times, dwell times, per-session energy demand, and
the vehicle fleet come from measured datasets, and the electricity price is the real
Netherlands day-ahead series. We document which quantities are real and which are modeled, and
we provide an adapter to the public Caltech ACN-Data session dataset as an independent
US calibration \cite{lee2019acndata}.

\paragraph{Tiers as observable conditions, not an abstract multiplier.}
A real charging operator does not infer a driver's income and ration by it, which would be
operationally strange and legally fraught, and we do not model it that way. What operators run
is differentiated pricing, memberships and subscription tiers, product tiers such as fast
versus level two, and dynamic and congestion pricing, each carrying a different per-kilowatt
hour margin. Drivers sort into these tiers by budget and by their charging conditions. The
defensible reading of the tier, and the one we adopt, is that it is an observable proxy for a
driver's charging situation, in particular reliance on public and fast charging versus access
to cheap home charging. Home-charging access is not evenly distributed, and the gradient is
documented. Most charging is done at home today, but even under full electrification roughly a
quarter of vehicles are projected to lack a reliable home charger \cite{ge2021}. Access splits by housing tenure, eighty-one percent of owner-occupied units have
a garage or carport against thirty-nine percent of renter-occupied units \cite{doeeere2018}, by
dwelling type, about ninety-three percent home-charging access for single-family homes against
about forty-six percent for apartments in 2020 \cite{bauer2021}, and by income, access in
lower-income communities is projected to fall to about fifty-nine percent by 2030 against about
seventy percent for the general population \cite{bauer2021}. It also splits by race.
California block groups where Black and Hispanic residents together form the majority have about
seven-tenths the odds of any public charger and about half the odds of a publicly funded one,
controlling for income and housing \cite{hsu2021}, disadvantaged communities nationally have
sixty-four percent fewer public
chargers per capita and seventy-three percent fewer once adjusted for multi-dwelling-unit renters
\cite{yu2025}, and sixty-three percent of Black against thirty percent of white households are
renters \cite{lou2024,khan2022}. Those without home charging also pay a per-kilowatt-hour premium
of roughly two to three times, public fast charging averaging about forty cents per kilowatt hour
\cite{borlaug2026} against a residential average near seventeen cents \cite{eia2025}. So the driver
stuck in the low-margin public-reliant tier is disproportionately the renter, the apartment
resident, the lower-income and the non-white driver, which is an empirical claim about real
charging conditions rather than a parameter we set.

\paragraph{Anchoring the magnitudes.}
We tie the per-tier margins to a documented affordability structure so the numbers are not
chosen for convenience. Each tier corresponds to a household income tercile, taken from the 2024
American Community Survey, whose median household income is about eighty-two thousand dollars and
whose tercile boundaries fall near fifty-four and one hundred twenty-two thousand dollars, with
representative within-tercile incomes of about thirty-five, eighty-two, and one hundred sixty-one
thousand dollars \cite{acs2024}. Each tier's margin is its willingness to pay per kilowatt hour
relative to the median household under a sub-proportional income elasticity, because charging is a
quasi-necessity and willingness to pay grows more slowly than income. Residential energy is
empirically a necessity, its income elasticity lies strictly between zero and one across studies,
with a central cluster around zero point three to zero point six \cite{schulte2017,espey2004}, and
we use zero point six, near the center of that range. The per-tier margins are the output of this
derivation from the income terciles and the elasticity, approximately zero point six, one, and one
point five for the budget, mid, and premium tiers, and as the next section shows only their
ordering, not their magnitudes, drives the result. The energy-burden
gradient corroborates the direction, low-income households below twice the poverty line spend a
median eight point one percent of income on home energy against two point three percent for
higher-income households, a factor of roughly three and a half \cite{drehobl2020}, so the
low-margin tier both sorts into the cheaper products and has materially lower marginal willingness
to pay for discretionary charging. The income terciles, the elasticity, and the energy-burden
figures carry real uncertainty, but the next section shows that uncertainty does not reach the
finding, because the disparate impact depends on the ordering these numbers produce and not on
their magnitudes.

\paragraph{The disparate impact is structural, not a calibration artifact.}
The elasticity is the estimated quantity that sets how far apart the tier margins are, and a
naive reading would treat a sweep over it as a sensitivity check that the finding happens to
pass. It is not a sensitivity check, and presenting it as one would concede too much. Table
\ref{tab:grounding} and Figure~\ref{fig:grounding} show that as the elasticity ranges across its
entire plausible interval the day-averaged tier gap does not drift, it holds near $0.567$ to
within solver tie-breaking, the
same value as the status-quo oracle in Table~\ref{tab:oracle}, and the lowest-paying tier is the
worst-served on ninety-seven percent of days at every value. A flat line is not a
sensitivity curve. It is the signature of a structural mechanism, which we state directly and
then establish in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}. Under a linear revenue objective and binding
scarcity, the profit optimum routes power up the willingness-to-pay ordering and starves the
lowest-ranked tier, and this depends only on the tiers' \emph{ordering}, not on how far apart
their margins sit. The elasticity sets only the magnitudes, so it moves only the revenue price
of fairness, which rises smoothly from about three to about thirteen percent, and never the
disparate impact itself. The harm is therefore not an artifact a different elasticity could
remove, it is a structural consequence of two facts that are not in dispute, that a
revenue-maximizing operator ranks tiers by margin and that income sorts drivers into those
tiers. It vanishes only at zero spread, where there is no ordering to exploit, which is the
correct and only-if behavior.

\paragraph{From tier to income, a translation and not a finding.}
The gap above is a disparity across price tiers. To read it as a disparate impact across income
we must carry it through the tier-to-income correlation, and we are careful to present that step
as translation rather than as evidence. Under a symmetric mixing model with correlation strength
$\rho$, the income-level gap is exactly $\rho$ times the tier-level gap, so at any assumed
correlation the budget-income bracket is the worst-served and the harm is a fixed fraction of the
tier gap (lower rows of Table~\ref{tab:grounding}). This is arithmetic, and it would be circular
to offer it as proof that the correlation is real. The empirical weight for that claim sits in
the charging-access literature of Section~\ref{sec:grounding}, that home-charging access and
public reliance track tenure, dwelling type, income, and race
\cite{ge2021,bauer2021,hsu2021,borlaug2026}. The mixing model only says how strongly that
documented correlation carries the
measured tier-level harm into income space.

\paragraph{Per-driver measure.}
For a driver $i$ we define satisfaction as the fraction of requested energy delivered by the
time the driver leaves,
\begin{equation}
u_i = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\text{delivered}_i}{\text{desired}_i},\,0,\,1\right),
\end{equation}
evaluated for every driver in the realized stream and not only for those who left satisfied. A
driver who hits the deadline half charged keeps that low score, a driver still plugged in at
the end of the day is counted by flushing the station at the horizon, and a driver turned away
because no charger was free receives a zero. Counting the rejected and the undercharged is what
stops an allocation from looking fair by serving only the easy cases or by turning the needy
away, and it is the measure on which every finding below is computed.

\section{The audit instrument}

To separate the difficulty of the problem from the difficulty of any particular controller, we
audit with a clairvoyant offline solver rather than with a heuristic. Given a realized day, the
actual stream of drivers with their arrival times, dwell windows, desired energy, and power
caps, we solve a linear program for the best achievable allocation with full foreknowledge of
the arrivals, subject to the binding grid-power limit, the per-car power caps, and the per-car
energy demand. The solver can maximize any of the objectives named in Section~2, revenue,
total satisfaction, or the worst-off tier, so the audit compares the allocations those
commitments actually produce on the same day rather than arguing about them in the abstract.

One point of correctness matters enough to state, because it changes the headline. A single
program that maximizes the worst-off level returns an allocation that levels the better-off
tiers down to that level, which reports a perfectly equal but needlessly impoverished outcome.
We instead solve two stages, the first maximizes the worst-off tier and the second holds that
value fixed and maximizes total delivered satisfaction, so the returned allocation is Pareto
efficient and its worst tier can never fall below what the revenue optimum already delivers.
The corrected instrument is what lets us say the price of fairness is modest, an earlier
leveling-down solver would have overstated it as a large cut in service. Because the linear
program relaxes the nonlinear charge curve and the battery round trip, its optimum is a valid
upper bound on what any causal controller could achieve on that day, which is the ceiling we
audit against.

\section{Findings}

The numbers below come from the released run on the scarce reference site, sixteen charger
connectors behind a thirty-kilowatt grid, with the grounded tiers. We report a tier's
satisfaction as its mean over drivers and days, and the tier gap as the difference between the
best and worst tier on that day-averaged satisfaction, which measures whether a tier is
\emph{systematically} underserved rather than unlucky on a given day. A per-day audit over
thirty-two days confirms the reading. Under the status-quo tariff the same tier, the
lowest-paying one, is the worst-served on thirty-one of thirty-two days, so the harm is
systematic and the day-averaged and per-day measures agree. Under an income-neutral tariff the
worst-served tier instead roams across the three tiers, so no tier is systematically starved
and the averaged gap collapses to noise. The measure agrees with the per-day picture exactly
when the harm is systematic and diverges exactly when it is noise, which is the behavior a
disparate-impact measure should have.

\paragraph{The price of fairness is a revenue cost, not a service cost.}
Table~\ref{tab:oracle} and Figure~\ref{fig:oracle} report the solver. The revenue-optimal allocation gives the mid and
premium tiers most of what they ask for and holds the budget tier near thirty-eight percent, a
tier gap of about $0.57$. The corrected maximin equalizes the three tiers at about $0.82$ while
delivering the \emph{same total energy} as the revenue optimum. That equality is exact rather
than average. On every one of the thirty-two days the objectives deliver identical total energy
to within solver tolerance, because the grid is the binding constraint, so the objectives only
redistribute a fixed quantity and never change how much is served. Moving from the revenue
optimum to the maximin optimum therefore costs margin-weighted revenue, a median of about nine
percent with a bootstrap interval of about eight to twelve percent, while mean satisfaction
rises from about $0.71$ to about $0.82$ and the worst tier rises from about $0.38$ to about
$0.82$. We are careful not to sell the aggregate rise as free.
It is the classical consequence of saturating charging utility, a driver does not want more
than a full battery, so energy moved from the near-full premium tier to the far-from-full
budget tier buys more satisfaction than it costs, and the premium tier does give up about
thirteen points of satisfaction to fund the redistribution. The honest statement is that the tension is
between the operator's revenue and the distribution across drivers, not between total service
and equity.

\paragraph{The mechanism is rank position, not spread magnitude.}
\label{sec:mechanism}
This is the central and most policy-relevant finding, and it corrects a natural but wrong
intuition, that the harm scales with how far apart the tier margins are. It does not. Table
\ref{tab:tariff} and Figure~\ref{fig:tariff} recompute the profit-optimal allocation under four
tariffs on one fixed day set, and the pattern is governed by rank, not spread. Under the revenue objective the operator
serves tiers in willingness-to-pay order, so the budget tier is systematically starved exactly
while it is the strictly lowest-paying rank, and by how much it is lowest does not enter. The two
intuitive partial fixes then behave asymmetrically in a way spread magnitude cannot explain.
Capping the premium tier down to the middle margin compresses the spread to $0.4$ but leaves the
budget tier still uniquely lowest, so the gap barely moves, from $0.57$ to $0.53$, and the budget
tier stays worst-served. Subsidizing the budget tier up to parity with the middle compresses the
spread only to $0.5$, a \emph{larger} spread than the premium cap, yet the gap drops to $0.32$,
because the budget tier is no longer uniquely lowest. The smaller-spread intervention leaves the
larger harm. Because both tariffs are evaluated on the same realized days, we can put an interval
on that contrast directly rather than on the two gaps separately. The paired difference is
$0.21$ with a $95\%$ bootstrap interval of $[0.15, 0.27]$, and it is positive in every one of
ten thousand resampled day sets, so the ordering is not an artifact of which thirty-two days we
drew. That is decisive, the lever is not the size of the spread but whether the budget
tier is rescued from being the unique bottom rank. The subsidy to parity does not remove the harm
either, it relocates it, the worst-served tier flips from the budget tier to the middle tier,
which the premium tier now outranks alone. Only removing the ordering entirely, an income-neutral
tariff, drives the systematic gap to essentially zero, about $0.05$ on this thirty-two-day set and
shrinking toward zero on longer horizons, so no tier is systematically starved. What flattening
does not remove is the inequality scarcity itself produces, a per-day gap near $0.17$ that lands on
a rotating tier, a within-population harm that only added capacity reaches. The policy
rule is therefore sharp and counterintuitive. Lift the lowest tier out of being uniquely lowest,
and to remove the harm rather than relocate it, remove the ordering itself by pricing income
neutrally. Capping the top tier changes the spread without changing who is at the bottom, and so
does almost nothing.

\paragraph{The harm is specific to power-scarce sites.}
Our headline is established on one site, so we re-run the audit at genuinely different stations,
varying charger count, grid size, arrival rate, dwell profile, and vehicle fleet, and we
re-run the tariff lever at each. One of those sites is not a variation of our own calibration at
all. It is driven by the public Caltech ACN-Data release, twenty-seven thousand real charging
sessions recorded at a US campus network, from which we take the arrival rate and time of day,
the dwell time, and the driver-stated energy demand. That site is an independent check on
whether the mechanism is an artifact of the European demand profiles the rest of the study is
calibrated on. The harm does not hold everywhere, and the boundary is
between a systematic and a rotating disadvantage (Figure~\ref{fig:scarcity}). At the three
power-bound sites the harm is
systematic, the same budget tier is the worst-served on ninety-seven, eighty-four and seventy-eight
percent of days and the day-averaged tier gap is about $0.57$ at the scarce residential reference,
$0.29$ at the ACN-Data campus site and $0.22$
at a high-traffic highway site, with a revenue price of fairness near nine, four and six percent. At a
workplace site with long spread dwells and a low-traffic shopping site, power does not bind, and
the disparate impact does not form, the worst-served tier rotates roughly evenly across the three
tiers day to day rather than settling on one, so the day-averaged tier gap is about $0.005$ and
$0.03$. We are careful about what this does and does not say. It says there is no \emph{systematic}
between-tier harm at these sites, no tier is persistently starved, which is what the disparate
impact requires. It does not say the sites are equal. The low-traffic shopping site still carries
substantial day-level inequality, a per-day gap near $0.19$, but that inequality lands on a
different tier each day, so it is a within-population effect rather than a between-tier disparate
impact, and it is the distinct harm the grid-capacity lever addresses. The revenue price of
fairness tracks the systematic harm across sites, it is positive exactly when rationing by tier is
profitable, which requires power to bind, and wherever the systematic harm exists the rank
mechanism holds, an income-neutral tariff removes more than ninety percent of the gap while a
budget-only subsidy leaves most of it. On the ACN-Data site those figures are ninety-five percent
against fifty-one percent, so the mechanism survives a change of continent, of demand data, and
of the sampling scheme that generated it. The rank asymmetry survives there too. Capping the
premium tier leaves a gap of $0.28$ while the larger-spread budget subsidy leaves $0.14$, a paired
difference of $0.14$ with a bootstrap interval of $[0.09, 0.20]$. One thing does differ, and we
report it rather than smooth it. At the reference site the subsidy to parity relocates the
shortfall onto the middle tier, which is worst-served in eighty-eight percent of resampled day
sets. On the ACN-Data site the same intervention leaves the budget tier marginally worst-served
instead, at $0.84$ against the middle tier's $0.86$, and that attribution holds in eighty-four
percent of resamples. So which tier ends up at the bottom after a budget-only subsidy is
site-specific and, on the ACN-Data site, close enough to be nearly a tie. Relocation onto the
middle tier is therefore not a general consequence of the subsidy. What does generalize, and what
the policy claim rests on, is the weaker statement that a budget-only subsidy leaves most of the
harm in place and only removing the ordering reaches the floor. The scoped claim we support is therefore that
revenue-optimal allocation \emph{systematically} disadvantages lower-income drivers exactly where
power binds and a strict price ordering exists, which is where the policy question actually lives,
and the scoping lets a regulator target the remedy at the sites a positive revenue price of
fairness identifies rather than mandate it everywhere.

\paragraph{Grid capacity is the second lever, and it works differently.}
Enough grid capacity removes the scarcity that forces power rationing. In the sweep
(Figure~\ref{fig:capacity}), the between-tier disparate impact under the oracle falls to
essentially zero by about sixty kilowatts for this sixteen-charger site, so added power removes the
harm this paper audits. The within-population inequality that the deployed max-charge policy
actually produces falls too, but it flattens at a nonzero floor near $0.30$, because a larger grid
connection removes power rationing and does not add chargers, and the residual comes from drivers
rejected when every charger is already busy, a scarcity that grid power does not touch. The oracle
shows a much lower within-population inequality is achievable with the same power, so that floor is
an online-to-offline and charger-provisioning gap rather than a capacity limit, and it is where a
better online controller would help (appendix). The pricing and capacity levers are also not
orthogonal (Figure~\ref{fig:levers}), and the asymmetry is worth stating precisely rather than
asserting a clean split. On the oracle allocation, capacity attacks the root cause and reduces
\emph{both} the between-tier gap, by about $0.55$, and the within-tier inequality, by about $0.23$,
as the connection rises from thirty to ninety kilowatts, whereas income-neutral pricing at fixed
capacity removes almost all of the between-tier disparate impact, about $0.52$, while barely moving
the within-tier inequality, about $0.07$. The practical reading is therefore asymmetric. Where the
grid can be sized up, capacity resolves the disparate impact and the power-scarcity part of the
within-population inequality, and where it cannot, income-neutral pricing removes the disparate
impact specifically. This turns the audit into a capacity-planning target as well as a pricing
recommendation.

\section{Stakeholders, governance, and the path to practice}
\label{sec:practice}

An audit is only socio-technical if it names who is harmed, who can act, and through which
instrument, so we do that explicitly rather than leave the finding in the simulator.

\paragraph{Who is harmed.}
The people underserved by revenue-optimal rationing are the drivers in the public-reliant
low-margin tier, disproportionately renters and multi-unit-dwelling residents without home
charging, lower-income households, and, through the documented correlation of charging access
with race, drivers of color \cite{hsu2021,khan2022}. The harm is concrete, a systematically
lower fraction of the energy they came to get, which for a driver without a home charger is not
an inconvenience but a constraint on whether the vehicle is usable the next day.

\paragraph{Who holds the levers.}
The decision-makers are charging network operators, who set the tariff and run the scheduler,
distribution utilities, whose rate designs and low-income programs the charging tariff sits
inside, and public utility commissions and regulators, who oversee pricing and can attach equity
conditions to charging procurement and to grid interconnection. Site planners and the public
programs that fund charging deployment hold the capacity lever.

\paragraph{The instruments the levers map to.}
The audit informs three real instruments. First, rate regulation, our central recommendation is
that public charging under scarcity be priced so that no tier is left strictly lowest, in the
limit income-neutrally, because removing the price ordering is the single intervention that
removes the systematic disadvantage, and a regulator who cannot mandate a flat tariff can require
the lowest tier to be lifted to parity, which is the general form of the same lever. A partial
compression that leaves the ordering intact, such as a top-tier cap, does not work. Second,
low-income charging tariffs and bill credits, which utilities already run for electricity and
which our audit says must be carried all the way to parity rather than left as a partial
poorest-tier credit, because a partial credit relocates the harm to the middle tier rather than
removing it. Third, grid connection sizing in charging procurement, since sizing above
the scarcity range removes the rationing that produces the harm in the first place. Because the
harm is specific to power-scarce sites, and because our audit flags those sites by a positive
revenue price of fairness, the intervention can be targeted, which makes it cheaper to fund and
easier to justify. The bill for the pricing remedy is a private revenue transfer of about ten
percent of margin-weighted revenue and no loss of total energy served, which bounds the size of
any credit a regulator would need to fund and is the number a rate case would turn on.

\paragraph{An operator can realize the fairer allocation online.}
Whether an operator must deploy a learned controller to act on this is a separate and smaller
question, because the actionable objects are the tariff and the connection, not the policy. For
completeness we checked whether a welfare-shaped online controller can realize the efficient,
high-service region the offline audit identifies, and it can. Trained on the inclusive measure
that counts a rejected driver as a zero, it beats the rule-based heuristics an operator would
otherwise run on profit, inclusive service, within-population inequality, and rejection at once,
on a majority of seeds, without winning by turning marginal drivers away. We report this as
evidence that the fairer operating point is reachable online and not as the contribution, and we
are explicit about its limit, the controller improves within-population service and scheduling
but does not by itself move the between-tier disparate impact, which remains the tariff's story.
The supporting detail, the training budget, the mechanism, and a negative result that the equity
weight is not a tunable between-tier fairness dial, is in the appendix, because it is
internal-validity machinery rather than part of the audit.

\section{Reflexivity and limitations}
\label{sec:reflexivity}

We are the analysts, not the affected community, and the audit reflects that. We have no
participatory input from the drivers the finding is about, no interviews, no account of how
renters without home charging would themselves frame the harm or rank the remedies, so the
audit speaks to the distributional dimension of energy justice and is silent on the procedural
and recognition dimensions. A finding that a group is underserved is not the same as that group
being heard, and we do not claim the second.

Modeling ability to pay carries its own risk, and we name it rather than hope it is not noticed.
A model that segments drivers by willingness to pay can be read as a tool for price
discrimination as easily as a tool for auditing its harm, and the same solver that computes the
maximin allocation computes the revenue-optimal one. We discuss this directly in the ethics
statement below and we frame the entire contribution around removing the spread that the harmful
optimum exploits, but we cannot prevent misuse by construction, and the honest position is that
the instrument is dual-use.

The gap between simulation and deployment is real. Our arrival, dwell, and demand distributions
are empirical but drawn from specific datasets, the tier-to-income mapping is grounded in public
data but is a model of demand rather than a measured demand curve from any one operator's
customers, and the offline solver relaxes the nonlinear charge curve so it is a valid but loose
upper bound. The robustness results are what let us claim the direction and structure of the
finding survive these gaps, but the precise magnitudes would move on a given operator's real
data, which is why we release the instrument for exactly that re-audit rather than present our
numbers as final.

\section{Conclusion}

We audited what revenue-optimal rationing does at a power-scarce charging site and found a
disparate impact on the drivers least able to charge at home, produced by a facially neutral
pricing structure rather than by any intent. The remedy is not the obvious one. A subsidy for
the poorest drivers relocates the harm, and because the harm tracks the tiers' price ordering
rather than the width of the spread, only removing the ordering, by pricing income-neutrally,
removes it, at a cost that falls on the operator's revenue and not on total service. Grid capacity
is a second lever that works differently, it attacks the scarcity itself and so reduces both the
between-tier harm and the within-population inequality that pricing alone leaves behind. The
finding is scoped to the sites where power binds, and it is structural rather than a calibration
artifact, depending on the tiers' ordering and not on the magnitudes we assume. We name the drivers who are
harmed, the operators and utilities and regulators who hold the levers, and the rate-design and
capacity instruments those levers map to, and we release the audit so those actors can run it on
their own data.

\section*{Ethics and adverse impacts statement}

This work audits a distributional harm in a real deployed setting, and the audit instrument is
dual-use, which is the primary adverse impact we must address. A model of ability-to-pay
allocation, of the kind we build to reveal the harm, is structurally the same object an operator
would build to optimize price discrimination against low-income drivers, and the revenue-optimal
allocation we compute as the thing to be corrected is exactly what an unconstrained profit
maximizer would deploy. We have three responses. First, our framing and every recommendation are
oriented toward removing the price ordering the harmful optimum exploits, so the contribution a
reader is handed is the remedy and the audit that justifies it, not a tuned discrimination engine.
Second, we deliberately keep segment membership exogenous and never infer a protected attribute at
allocation time, and our recommended instruments, income-neutral pricing, low-income tariffs, and
capacity sizing, act on pricing structure and infrastructure rather than on any inference of who
a driver is, which is both the more effective lever and the one that does not require profiling.
Third, we are explicit that the harm is real and current, operators already price by tier and
already ration under scarcity, so the risk of inaction is not neutral, and an audit that makes
the harm measurable and names the remedy is on balance protective even though the instrument
could be misused. We also flag a subtler risk, that quantifying ability to pay can reinforce the
framing that access to a basic service should track willingness to pay at all, and we take the
capability position, in Section~2, that access rather than payment is the right space precisely
to resist that framing. We use no human-subjects data, all datasets are public and
non-identifying, and the release contains no personal data.

\section{Realizing the fairer allocation online, supporting detail}

This appendix records the machinery behind the one paragraph in Section~\ref{sec:practice}
that reports the online controller. It is here rather than in the body because it is
internal-validity detail for the secondary question of online realizability, not part of the
audit.

\paragraph{Setup and result.}
We train a welfare-shaped policy with proximal policy optimization on a state-augmented
version of the environment, at an equity weight of one half, for three million steps over ten
seeds, and we judge it on the inclusive measure that counts a rejected driver as a zero. It is
compared against the rule-based heuristics an operator would deploy, not against a
profit-only learned run, which would share the training budget and confound the comparison.
Against the strongest heuristic it wins at the median on profit, inclusive satisfaction,
within-population inequality, and rejection rate, and it dominates on all four at once on seven
of ten seeds. Crucially it rejects \emph{fewer} drivers than the heuristic while scoring higher
on the measure that counts the rejected, so the service win is real and not an artifact of
shedding hard cases.

\paragraph{Mechanism.}
A three-condition ablation isolates what the controller needs. A content-free monotone
potential of matched magnitude fails, and a saturating-but-content-free potential fails the
same way, so neither dense reward nor saturating shape is sufficient. The gain requires the
demand-aware content of the welfare signal, the measure of how far each driver is from its own
charging target, which acts as a scheduling prior that completes the drivers who are behind.
Because that prior prioritizes whoever is behind on their own demand, as likely a premium
driver as a budget one, it does not track ability to pay, which is why it lifts
within-population service without moving the between-tier gap.

\paragraph{A negative result, stated plainly.}
The equity weight is not a tunable between-tier fairness dial. Across weights the between-tier
gap stays at seed noise, is not ordered by the weight, and the tier that ends up worst-served
is not even consistent across seeds. Online, both the profit and the welfare policies charge
broadly rather than ration hard, so the aggressive rationing that produces the offline
disparate impact never forms, and there is little there for the welfare objective to move. This
is why the between-tier disparate impact is the tariff's story told offline, while the
controller's contribution is efficiency and service told online. We report this negative result
rather than omit it, because a reader who runs the weight sweep will see it.
